Most explanations of graphs start too late.

They open with BFS, DFS, shortest paths, topological sort, and suddenly you are expected to care about a queue walking through letters named `A`, `B`, and `C`.

That is backwards.

Before graphs are an algorithm topic, they are a modeling topic.

A graph is what you reach for when the important part of the data is not just the items, but the relationships between the items.

This series is my attempt to make that idea feel obvious.

## The Story

The strange thing about graphs is that they are both the topic and the shape of the topic.

Graphs are made of connected things. A good learning path is also made of connected things.

So instead of treating this as one giant article, I am splitting it into smaller pieces that link to each other:

```txt
Graphs for Programmers
  -> Why graphs exist
  -> The math root
  -> Graphs compared to other structures
  -> Representing graphs in code
  -> Walking graphs
  -> Cycle detection
  -> Dependency order
  -> Weighted paths
  -> Search spaces
```

The goal is not to memorize graph algorithms like spell names.

The goal is to learn how to look at a messy system and ask:

```txt
What are the things?
How are the things connected?
What can I learn by walking those connections?
```

## The First Picture

Here is the shape we keep coming back to:

<Mermaid
  title="A graph is a model of things and relationships."
  chart={`
graph LR
  A[Thing] --> B[Related thing]
  A --> C[Another related thing]
  C --> D[Something reachable later]
`}/>

That picture could be a package dependency graph, a social network, a route map, a compiler model, a chess search tree, or a reading path.

The diagram does not know what the nodes mean.

You decide that.

That is the main design move in graph thinking:

```txt
node = what kind of thing?
edge = what kind of relationship?
question = what do I need to know?
```

The algorithm comes after that.

## Where To Start

If you are new to graphs, read this story in order.

Start with the reason graphs exist at all. Then learn the small amount of math vocabulary. Then compare graphs with arrays, maps, tables, and trees. Only after that should traversal start to feel useful.

That order matters because BFS and DFS are not the foundation.

They are ways of walking a model after the model already makes sense.

## The Learning Contract

Each article in this story should answer one question clearly.

If an article cannot do that, it should probably be split again.

That gives us a useful rule for writing technical essays:

```txt
one article = one conceptual move
one story = a connected path through many conceptual moves
```

Graphs happen to be the perfect first topic for this structure.

We are going to explain graphs by building a graph of explanations.
